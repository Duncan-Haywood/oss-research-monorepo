# Deadband twin: what does a linear actuator twin get wrong when the real actuator has a deadband, and why does it look fine on its own data?

Pure Python, no dependencies. The real actuator is `y = g·D(u) + w`, where `D` is a deadband of half-width `δ` (zero output for `|u| ≤ δ`, slope 1 outside, shifted to be continuous). The twin is linear, `y = b·u`, fitted by least squares on logs with Gaussian excitation of scale `s`. The fitted gain is exactly the probability that the excitation leaves the deadband, `b → g·P(|z| > δ/s) = g·2Q(δ/s)` (0.6167 vs 0.6171 at δ/s = 0.5; 0.3167 vs 0.3173 at 1; 0.0456 vs 0.0455 at 2). The fit residual is exactly uncorrelated with the input (~1e-14) and the fit R² is 0.89 at δ/s = 0.5, so nothing on the twin's own data warns. But the gain only holds at the excitation it was fitted at: a twin fitted at s = 1 over-predicts the real gain 13.5× at s = 0.25 and its held-out R² falls to −1.90 (0.89 at s = 1), and it under-predicts it at large scale (0.77× at s = 2, 0.68× at s = 4). A feedforward command `u = r/b` produces exactly zero output for targets below `δ·b = 0.31` and over-shoots by 1.12× / 1.37× / 1.52× at r = 1 / 2 / 5 (1/b ≈ 1.62 is right only near the fitted scale). In closed loop, `x ← x + D(k(r−x))` (the twin promises zero error for any 0 < k < 2) stalls at a final error of up to exactly `δ/k` (0.50 at k = 1, 0.26 at k = 1.9), and at k = 1 the final error is exactly `min(|e0|, δ)`. Fitting `(g, δ)` instead of a single gain recovers both (g = 1.003, δ = 0.502 at n = 100) and predicts at s = 0.25 to the noise floor (RMSE/sd(y) 0.95 vs 1.67 for the linear twin, noise floor ≈ 0.96). See `paper/whitepaper.md`.

**Builds on.** The digital-twin simulation-fidelity and sim-to-real direction of ARPG (<https://arpg.colorado.edu/>), the Autonomous Systems IRT (<https://www.colorado.edu/irt/autonomous-systems/>) and the manipulation sim-to-real interests of the HIRO Group (<https://hiro-group.ronc.one/>); no specific paper from those groups is reproduced and nothing here is affiliated with or endorsed by them. The Gaussian-input linearisation (Bussgang 1952; Price 1958) and the describing function (Gelb & Vander Velde 1968) are classical; the contribution is the twin-specific accounting. Companion to `control-twin`, `eiv-twin` and `saturation-twin` in this repository, which cover a wrong gain, a gain biased by input noise and a clipped actuator.

Stylised: scalar static actuator, Gaussian excitation, known-form deadband with symmetric width, a simulated "real" system, no dynamics, hysteresis or field data. MIT.
